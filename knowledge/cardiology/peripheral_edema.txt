Leg Swelling (Peripheral Edema) — Clinical Reference

Leg swelling is a common complaint with a wide range of causes, from
prolonged sitting to serious cardiac, kidney, liver, or vascular disease.
A cardiology consultation should focus on distinguishing heart failure and
venous disease from other causes and identifying red flags.

Useful history features:
- Laterality: bilateral (both legs) swelling more often suggests a
  systemic cause such as heart failure, kidney disease, liver disease, or
  medication side effects. Unilateral (one leg) swelling, especially if
  sudden and painful, raises concern for deep vein thrombosis (DVT), which
  is an emergency.
- Onset: gradual swelling over weeks fits chronic venous insufficiency or
  early heart failure. Sudden swelling over hours to a day or two,
  particularly one-sided, is more concerning for DVT.
- Associated symptoms: shortness of breath, orthopnea, and fatigue
  alongside bilateral leg swelling point toward heart failure. Pain,
  warmth, and redness in one leg point toward DVT or cellulitis. Abdominal
  swelling and yellowing of the skin/eyes point toward liver disease.
- Timing pattern: swelling that is worse at the end of the day and
  improves overnight is typical of venous or dependent edema. Swelling
  that is present on waking and does not fully resolve suggests a more
  systemic process such as heart failure or kidney disease.
- Medications: calcium channel blockers, NSAIDs, and steroids are common
  medication causes of leg swelling worth asking about.

Red flags requiring urgent evaluation:
- Sudden, painful, unilateral leg swelling (possible DVT), especially with
  new shortness of breath or chest pain (possible pulmonary embolism)
- Leg swelling with significant shortness of breath, orthopnea, or
  paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea (possible decompensated heart failure)
- Swelling with skin breakdown, spreading redness, warmth, and fever
  (possible cellulitis or infection)
- Rapid, severe swelling in someone with known kidney or liver disease

Cardiac mechanism: in heart failure, reduced pumping efficiency leads to
increased pressure in the veins, which pushes fluid out into the tissues,
typically starting in the most dependent areas (the ankles and lower legs
when upright). This is usually bilateral and accompanied by other signs of
fluid overload such as weight gain and breathlessness.

Non-cardiac causes to keep in mind: chronic venous insufficiency (weak leg
vein valves, often with varicose veins and improvement when legs are
elevated), lymphedema, kidney disease (nephrotic syndrome causing
significant protein loss and swelling), liver disease with low protein
production, hypothyroidism, and simple dependent edema from prolonged
sitting or standing, pregnancy, or certain medications.

Typical workup when a cardiac cause is suspected: examination for other
signs of fluid overload, an ECG, a BNP/NT-proBNP blood test, basic kidney
and liver function tests, and an echocardiogram if heart failure is a
realistic concern. If DVT is suspected, urgent vascular imaging (leg
Doppler ultrasound) is needed rather than routine outpatient follow-up.
