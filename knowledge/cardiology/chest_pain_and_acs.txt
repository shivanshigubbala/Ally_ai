Chest Pain and Acute Coronary Syndrome — Clinical Reference

Chest pain is the single most important symptom a cardiology triage
conversation must characterize carefully, because it can range from a
harmless musculoskeletal strain to a life-threatening heart attack.

Key features to establish in a history:
- Location: central/retrosternal chest pain is more concerning for cardiac
  origin than pain that is sharply localized to one small spot with the
  fingertip, which is more often musculoskeletal.
- Character: pressure, tightness, squeezing, or a heavy weight on the chest
  is classic for cardiac ischemia. Sharp, stabbing pain that changes with
  breathing or position is more typical of pleuritic or musculoskeletal
  causes.
- Radiation: pain spreading to the left arm, both arms, the jaw, neck, or
  between the shoulder blades raises concern for a cardiac cause.
- Associated symptoms: shortness of breath, sweating (diaphoresis), nausea,
  vomiting, or a sense of impending doom alongside chest pain are red flags
  for acute coronary syndrome (ACS), which includes unstable angina and
  heart attack (myocardial infarction).
- Triggers and relief: pain brought on by exertion and relieved by rest is
  classic for stable angina. Pain occurring at rest, waking someone from
  sleep, or lasting more than 15-20 minutes is more concerning and may
  indicate an evolving heart attack rather than stable angina.
- Duration and pattern: brief (seconds), fleeting, needle-like pains are
  rarely cardiac. Pain lasting many hours without any change is less likely
  to be an acute coronary event but can still reflect other serious cardiac
  or non-cardiac pathology and should not be dismissed.

Emergency red flags requiring immediate emergency care rather than routine
consultation:
- Crushing or severe chest pain, especially with radiation to the arm, jaw,
  or back
- Chest pain with profuse sweating, nausea, or vomiting
- Chest pain with fainting or a sense of collapse
- Severe, sudden shortness of breath accompanying chest discomfort
- Chest pain in a patient with known coronary artery disease, diabetes, or
  prior heart attack that feels similar to a previous cardiac event

Risk factors that raise the pre-test probability of a cardiac cause:
older age, male sex, smoking, diabetes, hypertension, high cholesterol,
family history of premature coronary artery disease, obesity, and sedentary
lifestyle.

Typical initial workup when ACS is suspected: a 12-lead ECG to look for
ST-segment changes, cardiac troponin blood testing to detect heart muscle
injury, and often a chest X-ray and echocardiogram to assess heart function
and rule out other causes. Ongoing or worsening chest pain of cardiac
character always warrants urgent in-person evaluation rather than
reassurance over a chat interface.

Non-cardiac causes worth keeping in mind so the consultation does not
over-medicalize benign pain: costochondritis (tenderness reproducible by
pressing on the chest wall), gastroesophageal reflux (burning pain related
to meals or lying down), muscle strain after exertion or coughing, and
anxiety/panic attacks (often with hyperventilation, tingling, and a rapid
onset tied to a stressful trigger). These should still be asked about
directly rather than assumed, and red flags always take precedence over a
benign-sounding story.
