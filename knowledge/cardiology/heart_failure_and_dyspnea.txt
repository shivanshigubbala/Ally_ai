Heart Failure and Shortness of Breath (Dyspnea) — Clinical Reference

Shortness of breath is a common presenting symptom with both cardiac and
non-cardiac causes. In a cardiology context, the priority is distinguishing
heart failure and other cardiac causes from pulmonary or other causes.

Key history features suggesting a cardiac cause:
- Exertional dyspnea that has progressively worsened over days to weeks
- Orthopnea: breathlessness when lying flat, relieved by sitting up or
  propping on extra pillows
- Paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea: waking suddenly at night gasping for air
- Rapid weight gain over days (fluid retention) alongside breathlessness
- Swelling in the ankles or legs developing alongside the breathlessness
- A history of prior heart attack, longstanding hypertension, valvular
  heart disease, or a known weak heart muscle (cardiomyopathy)

Key features suggesting a non-cardiac or alternative cause:
- Fever, productive cough, and breathlessness pointing toward a
  respiratory infection such as pneumonia
- Wheeze and a history of asthma or COPD suggesting an airway cause
- Sudden-onset, severe breathlessness with one-sided chest pain and leg
  swelling raising concern for pulmonary embolism, which is itself an
  emergency
- Breathlessness tied closely to anxiety, with tingling around the mouth
  or fingers and a clear situational trigger

Red flags requiring urgent or emergency evaluation:
- Severe shortness of breath at rest or breathlessness that prevents
  speaking in full sentences
- Shortness of breath with chest pain, fainting, or blue-tinged lips
- Sudden-onset severe breathlessness, especially with one-sided leg
  swelling or pain (possible pulmonary embolism)
- Known heart failure with rapidly worsening symptoms despite usual
  medications

Heart failure basics: heart failure means the heart is not pumping or
filling efficiently enough to meet the body's needs. It can result from
weakened squeezing function (reduced ejection fraction) or from a stiff
heart that does not relax and fill properly (preserved ejection fraction).
Common causes include prior heart attacks, longstanding high blood
pressure, valve disease, and cardiomyopathies. Hallmark symptoms are
exertional breathlessness, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea,
fatigue, and fluid retention (leg swelling, rapid weight gain, abdominal
bloating).

Typical workup: a chest X-ray to look for fluid in the lungs or an
enlarged heart silhouette, BNP or NT-proBNP blood testing (a hormone
released when the heart is under strain, useful for supporting or
excluding a heart failure diagnosis), an ECG to look for prior heart
attack or rhythm abnormalities, and an echocardiogram to directly assess
heart muscle function and valve structure. Basic blood work (kidney
function, electrolytes, thyroid function, and a blood count to exclude
anemia) is also routinely useful, since anemia and thyroid disease can
both mimic or worsen breathlessness.
