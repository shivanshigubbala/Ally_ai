Syncope and Dizziness — Clinical Reference

Syncope is a transient, complete loss of consciousness and postural tone
with spontaneous full recovery, caused by temporarily reduced blood flow
to the brain. Dizziness is a broader, vaguer term that patients may use for
lightheadedness, a spinning sensation (vertigo), unsteadiness, or a feeling
of nearly passing out (presyncope). Distinguishing which the patient means
is an important first step.

Cardiac causes of syncope (higher concern, need prompt evaluation):
- Arrhythmias: both very fast (tachyarrhythmia) and very slow
  (bradyarrhythmia, heart block) rhythms can drop blood flow to the brain
  enough to cause fainting, often with little or no warning
- Structural heart disease: conditions such as aortic stenosis or
  hypertrophic cardiomyopathy can cause fainting, classically with
  exertion
- Pulmonary embolism or other causes of sudden reduced cardiac output

Non-cardiac (usually lower-risk) causes:
- Vasovagal (reflex) syncope: triggered by prolonged standing, pain, fear,
  the sight of blood, or a hot/crowded environment, often with a prodrome
  of nausea, sweating, and tunnel vision before fainting
- Orthostatic (postural) hypotension: fainting or lightheadedness on
  standing up quickly, especially in the elderly, the dehydrated, or those
  on blood pressure medication
- Situational syncope: triggered by coughing, urinating, or straining

Features that raise concern for a cardiac cause and warrant urgent
evaluation:
- Syncope during exertion rather than after standing still
- Syncope with no warning symptoms (sudden, "like a light switch")
- Syncope with chest pain, palpitations, or shortness of breath
- Syncope in someone with known heart disease or an abnormal ECG
- A family history of sudden cardiac death at a young age
- Syncope resulting in injury, or occurring while sitting or lying down

Reassuring features more typical of benign vasovagal syncope: a clear
trigger (prolonged standing, emotional stress, medical procedures), a
prodrome of warmth, sweating, and nausea beforehand, and quick, complete
recovery afterward without confusion.

Dizziness that is a spinning sensation (vertigo) rather than fainting is
usually related to the inner ear or the vestibular system rather than the
heart, though it is still worth briefly screening for cardiac red flags
(chest pain, palpitations, exertional trigger) since patients do not
always describe symptoms precisely.

Typical workup for suspected cardiac syncope: a 12-lead ECG is essential
in every case of true syncope to look for arrhythmia, conduction disease,
or signs of structural heart disease. Depending on the history, this may
be followed by ambulatory rhythm monitoring (Holter or longer-term event
monitor), an echocardiogram to assess structural heart disease, and
orthostatic blood pressure measurements (checking blood pressure lying
down and then standing) to evaluate for postural hypotension.
