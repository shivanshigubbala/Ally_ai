Hypertension (High Blood Pressure) — Clinical Reference

Hypertension is usually asymptomatic and discovered incidentally, but a
cardiology consultation should still assess for symptoms, complications,
and risk factors when a patient reports high blood pressure or blood
pressure-related symptoms.

Blood pressure categories commonly used as a reference (values in mmHg,
systolic/diastolic):
- Normal: below 120/80
- Elevated: systolic 120-129 and diastolic below 80
- Stage 1 hypertension: systolic 130-139 or diastolic 80-89
- Stage 2 hypertension: systolic 140 or higher, or diastolic 90 or higher
- Hypertensive crisis: systolic above 180 and/or diastolic above 120,
  which requires urgent evaluation, especially if accompanied by symptoms

Most people with hypertension have no symptoms at all, which is why it is
sometimes called a "silent" condition. When symptoms do occur, they are
often nonspecific: headache, dizziness, or nosebleeds, and these do not
reliably correlate with the actual blood pressure reading.

Symptoms that suggest a hypertensive emergency and require urgent care:
- Severe headache with blurred vision or confusion
- Chest pain or shortness of breath alongside very high blood pressure
- Severe headache with nausea/vomiting and a very high reading
- New weakness, numbness, or difficulty speaking (possible stroke)
- Any very high reading (systolic above 180 or diastolic above 120)
  accompanied by symptoms rather than an isolated number

History to gather: duration of known hypertension, current medications and
adherence, home blood pressure readings if available, salt intake, alcohol
use, caffeine intake, family history of hypertension or early
cardiovascular disease, and other cardiovascular risk factors (diabetes,
smoking, high cholesterol, obesity, sedentary lifestyle). It is also worth
asking about symptoms of secondary causes in younger patients or those with
resistant hypertension: episodic sweating and palpitations (pheochromocytoma),
snoring and daytime sleepiness (obstructive sleep apnea), or a history of
kidney disease.

Complications to be aware of: uncontrolled long-term hypertension is a
major driver of heart attack, stroke, heart failure, kidney disease, and
damage to the blood vessels of the eyes. This is why control matters even
though the condition itself is often silent.

Typical workup and monitoring: home or ambulatory blood pressure monitoring
to confirm the diagnosis and rule out "white coat" effect, basic blood
tests (kidney function, electrolytes, glucose, and a lipid profile) to
assess overall cardiovascular risk and screen for secondary causes, an ECG
to look for evidence of longstanding strain on the heart, and, in some
cases, an echocardiogram if there is concern for heart enlargement or
reduced function. Lifestyle counseling (reduced salt intake, regular
exercise, weight management, limiting alcohol, and smoking cessation) is a
core part of management alongside medication.
