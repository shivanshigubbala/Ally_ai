Palpitations and Arrhythmias — Clinical Reference

Palpitations are an uncomfortable awareness of the heartbeat — racing,
pounding, fluttering, or skipped beats. Most palpitations are benign, but a
careful history helps separate low-risk causes from arrhythmias that need
further testing.

Useful history to gather:
- Onset and offset: sudden start and sudden stop suggests a re-entrant
  arrhythmia such as supraventricular tachycardia (SVT). Gradual onset and
  offset is more typical of sinus tachycardia from anxiety, caffeine,
  exertion, fever, or dehydration.
- Regularity: a regular fast heartbeat suggests sinus tachycardia or SVT.
  An irregularly irregular rhythm raises concern for atrial fibrillation,
  especially in older patients or those with hypertension, diabetes, or
  thyroid disease.
- Single skipped beats or a "flip-flop" sensation are often benign
  premature atrial or ventricular contractions (PACs/PVCs), particularly
  if brief and infrequent, though frequent or symptomatic ectopy still
  merits an ECG.
- Duration: seconds versus sustained episodes lasting minutes to hours
  changes both differential diagnosis and urgency.
- Triggers: caffeine, alcohol, stimulant use, poor sleep, stress, and
  intense exercise are common benign triggers. Palpitations triggered by
  minimal exertion, or occurring at rest without a clear trigger, deserve
  more attention.
- Associated symptoms: chest pain, shortness of breath, dizziness,
  near-fainting, or actual fainting alongside palpitations are red flags
  and suggest a hemodynamically significant arrhythmia.

Red flags requiring urgent evaluation:
- Palpitations with fainting (syncope) or a sensation of nearly fainting
- Palpitations with chest pain or significant shortness of breath
- A very fast, sustained heart rate that does not resolve on its own
- Known structural heart disease, prior heart attack, or a family history
  of sudden cardiac death alongside new palpitations

Common underlying causes to consider: atrial fibrillation, supraventricular
tachycardia, frequent premature beats, thyroid dysfunction (both
hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism can cause palpitations), anemia,
electrolyte disturbances, medication or stimulant effects, anxiety and
panic disorder, and, less commonly, structural or electrical heart disease
such as cardiomyopathy or accessory conduction pathways.

Diagnostic approach: a resting 12-lead ECG is the first step and can be
diagnostic if the arrhythmia is present at the time of recording. Because
many arrhythmias are intermittent, ambulatory monitoring — a Holter monitor
(continuous recording over 24-48 hours) or a longer event monitor — is
often needed to capture an episode. Blood tests for thyroid function and
electrolytes, and an echocardiogram to assess for structural heart disease,
are commonly paired with rhythm monitoring when the history or ECG is
abnormal, when episodes are frequent, or when red-flag symptoms are
present.
