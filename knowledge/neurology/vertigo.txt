Vertigo and Dizziness — Clinical Reference

DEFINITION
Dizziness is a broad term encompassing vertigo, presyncope, disequilibrium, and non-specific dizziness. Vertigo is the specific illusion of motion — the sensation that self or environment is moving or rotating when there is none. It reflects a mismatch between vestibular, visual, and proprioceptive inputs.

CLASSIFICATION OF DIZZINESS
1. Vertigo: illusion of rotation/movement — usually vestibular cause
2. Presyncope: feeling of near faint — cardiac or vasovagal cause
3. Disequilibrium: imbalance, mainly when walking — central or peripheral
4. Non-specific dizziness: light-headedness, "floating" — anxiety, hyperventilation

PERIPHERAL vs CENTRAL VERTIGO

Peripheral vertigo:
- Origin: inner ear or vestibular nerve
- Causes: BPPV, vestibular neuritis, Meniere's disease
- Character: intense, episodic, positional, no neurological features
- Nystagmus: horizontal or torsional, fatigable, suppressed by fixation
- Hearing: often affected (loss, tinnitus)

Central vertigo:
- Origin: brainstem or cerebellum
- Causes: stroke, TIA, MS, tumor, migraine
- Character: may be mild but constant; can be positional
- Nystagmus: vertical, direction-changing, not suppressed by fixation
- Gait: severe ataxia
- Neurological signs: diplopia, dysphagia, facial numbness, weakness

RED FLAGS (central cause must be excluded)
- Sudden onset severe vertigo with headache ("thunderclap")
- New vertigo with neurological symptoms: diplopia, dysphagia, dysarthria, weakness, numbness
- Vertical or direction-changing nystagmus
- Inability to walk (severe gait ataxia)
- Risk factors: elderly, hypertension, atrial fibrillation, known vascular disease
- Headache, neck pain associated

BENIGN PAROXYSMAL POSITIONAL VERTIGO (BPPV)
- Most common cause of vertigo (up to 50%)
- Pathophysiology: otoconia (calcium carbonate crystals) displaced into semicircular canals
- Most common canal: posterior semicircular canal (PC-BPPV)

Clinical features:
- Brief (<1 min) episodic vertigo triggered by specific head movements
- Lying down, rolling over in bed, looking up ("top shelf"), bending forward
- No hearing loss or tinnitus

Diagnosis:
- Dix-Hallpike test (posterior canal): provokes upbeat-torsional nystagmus, fatigable
- Supine roll test (horizontal canal): horizontal nystagmus

Treatment:
- Particle repositioning maneuvers
- Epley maneuver (posterior canal BPPV): >80% effective
- Barbecue roll / Lempert maneuver (horizontal canal BPPV)
- Brandt-Daroff exercises (if maneuvers unsuccessful)
- Semont maneuver (alternative for posterior canal)

VESTIBULAR NEURITIS (NEURONITIS)
- Inflammation of vestibular nerve (often post-viral)
- Acute onset severe vertigo, nausea, vomiting lasting days to weeks
- No hearing loss (distinguishes from labyrinthitis where hearing is also affected)
- Labyrinthitis: same but WITH hearing loss (cochlear involvement)

Treatment:
- Acute phase: vestibular suppressants (meclizine, dimenhydrinate, prochlorperazine) — short-term only
- Antiemetics for vomiting
- Corticosteroids (prednisolone) may speed recovery
- Vestibular rehabilitation therapy (VRT) — key to long-term recovery

MENIERE'S DISEASE
Classic triad:
- Episodic vertigo attacks lasting 20 min to 12 hours
- Sensorineural hearing loss (fluctuating, low-frequency initially)
- Tinnitus and aural fullness

Pathophysiology: endolymphatic hydrops (excess endolymph in inner ear)

Treatment:
- Low-salt diet (<1500mg/day), avoid caffeine and alcohol
- Diuretics: hydrochlorothiazide, acetazolamide
- Betahistine (reduces endolymph pressure)
- Acute attacks: vestibular suppressants
- Intratympanic gentamicin (ablates vestibular function — unilateral)
- Intratympanic dexamethasone
- Endolymphatic sac surgery (rarely)

VESTIBULAR MIGRAINE
- Most common central cause of episodic vertigo
- Vertigo (often without headache) meeting migraine criteria
- Duration: 5 min to 72 hours
- Associated: photophobia, phonophobia, visual aura
- Treatment: migraine prophylaxis; triptans for acute attacks
- Diagnosis requires >5 episodes

STROKE/TIA (CENTRAL VERTIGO)
- PICA (posterior inferior cerebellar artery) infarct: Wallenberg syndrome
  - Ipsilateral face numbness, Horner's syndrome, ataxia
  - Contralateral body numbness, dysphagia, hiccups
- AICA (anterior inferior cerebellar artery): hearing loss + vertigo (labyrinthine artery)
- Important: isolated vertigo can rarely be the only stroke symptom
- HINTS exam: Head Impulse, Nystagmus, Test of Skew
  - Normal head impulse + direction-changing nystagmus + skew deviation = CENTRAL (stroke)

VESTIBULAR INVESTIGATIONS
- Audiometry (hearing assessment)
- Videonystagmography (VNG): electronically records nystagmus
- Caloric testing: irrigate ear with warm/cool water; assesses individual canal function
- Video Head Impulse Test (vHIT): measures VOR gain
- Rotational chair testing
- MRI brain (gadolinium): posterior fossa assessment
- VEMP (vestibular evoked myogenic potentials): otolith function

VESTIBULAR REHABILITATION
- Exercise-based therapy to promote central compensation
- Gaze stabilization exercises
- Balance training, habituation exercises
- Most beneficial for unilateral vestibular hypofunction
- Cawthorne-Cooksey exercises

TREATMENT PRINCIPLES
Vestibular suppressants (short-term only, max 3 days):
- Meclizine (antihistamine), dimenhydrinate
- Avoid long-term: delay central compensation

Antiemetics:
- Ondansetron, prochlorperazine, metoclopramide

Benzodiazepines (short-term acute):
- Lorazepam, diazepam
