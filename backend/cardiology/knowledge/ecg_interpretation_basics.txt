ECG Interpretation Basics — Clinical Reference

The electrocardiogram (ECG/EKG) records the heart's electrical activity
and is one of the fastest, most widely available tests in cardiology. This
reference summarizes what an ECG can show at a conceptual level — useful
for explaining to a patient why a test is being recommended, not for
generating an actual interpretation of a specific tracing.

What a standard 12-lead ECG can reveal:
- Heart rate and rhythm: whether the heart is beating too fast
  (tachycardia, generally over 100 beats per minute at rest), too slow
  (bradycardia, generally under 60 beats per minute), regular, or
  irregular (as in atrial fibrillation).
- Conduction abnormalities: delays or blocks in how the electrical signal
  travels through the heart (for example, various degrees of heart block,
  or bundle branch block).
- Signs of ischemia or prior heart attack: ST-segment elevation can
  indicate an acute, ongoing heart attack requiring emergency treatment.
  ST-segment depression or T-wave inversion can suggest reduced blood flow
  to part of the heart muscle. Q waves can indicate a prior, older heart
  attack.
- Chamber enlargement: certain voltage and wave patterns can suggest
  thickening or enlargement of the heart's chambers, often related to
  longstanding hypertension or valve disease.
- Electrolyte and other systemic effects: certain electrolyte
  abnormalities (such as high or low potassium) and some medications
  produce characteristic ECG changes.

Why an ECG is often the first test ordered: it is quick, painless,
noninvasive, and can be performed in almost any clinical setting. For
symptoms like chest pain, palpitations, or syncope, an ECG is usually the
first-line investigation because it can immediately identify
life-threatening findings (such as an evolving heart attack or a
dangerous arrhythmia) and helps determine whether further urgent workup is
needed.

Limitations: a single resting ECG only captures a brief snapshot in time.
Many arrhythmias are intermittent and will not show up unless they happen
to be occurring at the moment the ECG is recorded — this is why ambulatory
monitoring (Holter or event monitors) is used when episodic symptoms are
suspected to be arrhythmic but a resting ECG is normal. Similarly, a normal
ECG does not fully rule out coronary artery disease, especially between
episodes of chest pain, which is why troponin blood testing and other
investigations are often paired with it when acute coronary syndrome is a
concern.

How this fits into a consultation: when a patient describes chest pain,
palpitations, syncope, or significant risk factors for heart disease,
recommending a 12-lead ECG as a baseline test is almost always reasonable
and low-risk, since it is quick and noninvasive. It should be framed to
the patient as a first step to look for any electrical or structural
issues with the heart, not as confirmation that something is definitely
wrong.
