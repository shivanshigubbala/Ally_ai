Lipid Disorders and Cardiovascular Risk Factors — Clinical Reference

Dyslipidemia (abnormal cholesterol or triglyceride levels) is usually
asymptomatic and identified through blood testing, but it is one of the
most important modifiable drivers of atherosclerosis, heart attack, and
stroke, so a cardiology consultation should assess it as part of overall
risk even when it is not the chief complaint.

Key lipid measures on a standard panel:
- LDL cholesterol ("bad" cholesterol): the primary target for
  cardiovascular risk reduction. Higher levels drive plaque buildup in
  artery walls.
- HDL cholesterol ("good" cholesterol): helps remove cholesterol from
  the bloodstream; higher levels are generally protective.
- Triglycerides: another type of blood fat; very high levels are also
  associated with cardiovascular risk and, at extreme levels, with
  pancreatitis.
- Total cholesterol: the sum used in some risk calculators, but LDL and
  HDL individually are usually more clinically informative.

Modifiable cardiovascular risk factors to screen for in any cardiology
consultation:
- Smoking (including vaping) — one of the most powerful modifiable risk
  factors
- Hypertension
- Diabetes or prediabetes
- Dyslipidemia
- Obesity, particularly central/abdominal obesity
- Sedentary lifestyle
- Poor diet (high in saturated fat, sodium, and processed food)
- Excess alcohol intake
- Poor sleep and untreated sleep apnea
- Chronic stress

Non-modifiable risk factors: age, sex (men are generally at higher risk at
a younger age, though risk in women rises after menopause), and family
history of premature coronary artery disease (typically defined as a heart
attack or cardiac death in a first-degree male relative before age 55, or
a female relative before age 65).

Why this matters even without symptoms: cardiovascular risk factors are
cumulative and largely silent until they cause an event such as a heart
attack or stroke. A consultation that surfaces symptoms such as chest pain
or palpitations should still ask about smoking status, known cholesterol
or blood sugar levels, family history, and lifestyle, since these change
both the interpretation of symptoms and the recommended next steps.

Typical workup: a fasting or non-fasting lipid profile (LDL, HDL,
triglycerides, total cholesterol), blood glucose or HbA1c to screen for
diabetes, and blood pressure measurement. In patients with multiple risk
factors or an unclear risk level, a formal cardiovascular risk score may
be used to estimate 10-year risk of a cardiac event, which helps guide how
aggressively to treat.

Lifestyle guidance commonly given, alongside any medication a physician
may prescribe: a diet lower in saturated fat and sodium and higher in
fiber, fruits, and vegetables; regular aerobic exercise; smoking
cessation; limiting alcohol; weight management; and improving sleep
quality. These recommendations are appropriate to mention generally, but
specific medication choices and dosing are a physician's decision, not
something a triage conversation should determine.
